\begin{abstract}

As-Rigid-As-Possible deformation with Smooth Rotations (SR-ARAP \cite{Levi2015}) has recently gained popularity in applications that need an isometric surface mapping, because the quality of its results is comparable to that of more costly volumetric techniques operating on tetrahedral meshes. SR-ARAP minimizes a non-linear least-squares energy with a local/global optimization. Its local step estimates the rotation of each surface cell together with the rotations of its neighbors, so the rotations are codependent across the whole surface. The local step is currently solved by relaxation, and since only a few relaxation iterations can be afforded per step, the rotations it produces are approximate. We show that, with linearized quaternion rotations, the local step can be solved exactly. After a diagonal scaling it becomes a single sparse, symmetric positive definite linear system, which we prove to be the exact stationarity condition of a well-defined quadratic energy. A feedback term towards the previous rotations bounds the system's smallest eigenvalue independently of the mesh, which keeps it well conditioned. We prove that preconditioning the system with a Cholesky factorization of its position-independent part, which is factored once per set of constraints, bounds the condition number by a constant that shrinks as the mesh is refined. Conjugate gradients then reach the exact rotations, to $10^{-12}$~rad, in 5 to 20 iterations of linear cost, so the exact local step scales nearly linearly with the mesh and is up to 49 times faster than a direct factorization on large meshes. Compared with the original SR-ARAP local step, the converged shapes have 5--12\% lower ARAP distortion and smoother rotations, and they reach the quality at which SR-ARAP stops 2.1 to 4.3 times sooner. Putting the unit-norm constraint back, one cell at a time, shows what the linearization does: it freezes the Lagrange multiplier of the constraint, and the exact multiplier differs from the frozen one by the cell's distortion and the disagreement of its neighbors, which keeps the linearized rotations within $0.15^\circ$ of the exact ones. The same analysis explains why large twists converge slowly: a handle turned by $180^\circ$ has a rotation orthogonal, as a quaternion, to the rotations of its neighbors, and its cells stay behind; letting them take their own rotation in that case reaches the same shapes five times sooner. Combined with a multiresolution scheme that transfers the rotations by harmonic interpolation, the method deforms a 173{,}000-vertex model interactively, with the exact local steps taking 2.6~ms of a 30~ms frame.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\indent High-quality surface deformation aims to preserve the local properties of a shape as much as possible, removing distortion in the form of shear and stretch. As-Rigid-As-Possible deformation with Smooth Rotations (SR-ARAP) \cite{Levi2015} creates a surface mapping that minimizes the deviation from rigid behavior at the local scale. It also makes the rigid transformations vary smoothly over local neighborhoods, distributing distortion uniformly over the surface. The resulting deformations preserve the shape not only at small scales but also at large scales, which significantly increases the quality of the final result.

The current SR-ARAP method has two main disadvantages. The first is the local step, which looks for the best rotation of each surface cell while keeping neighboring rotations similar to each other. It needs to converge globally over the whole mesh. Currently the local step is solved with a relaxation method: the rotation of each cell is optimized while its neighbors' rotations are kept fixed, and the process is repeated until convergence. Although the cost of each relaxation iteration can be reduced considerably by avoiding SVD computations \cite{Ligare2020}, a fixed number of iterations is usually performed in order to bound the solver time. That compromises the quality of the results, since a fixed number of iterations (usually two or three) is not enough to reach optimal rotations across the surface. The second disadvantage is the high computational cost, which increases drastically with the number of vertices: SR-ARAP is a non-linear technique that requires several local/global iterations to converge.

In this paper we address both drawbacks. First, we solve the local step as a single linear system of equations, avoiding the need to solve a series of codependent SVD problems iteratively. We show that, after a diagonal scaling, this system is symmetric positive definite and is the exact stationarity condition of a well-defined quadratic energy (Section~\ref{section:exact_energy}). We bound its condition number and show that a preconditioner built from its position-independent part, factored once per set of constraints, makes conjugate gradients converge in a number of iterations that does not grow with the mesh (Section~\ref{section:pcg}); the exact local step therefore costs nearly linear time, and on the meshes we tested it reaches the quality of the original SR-ARAP sooner. Second, we propose a simple multiresolution method: the non-linear problem is first solved on a simplified surface mesh, and the optimized rotations are then transferred to the full-resolution mesh by harmonic interpolation, reusing the Laplacian matrix of the global step.

We also examine the linearization itself. Putting the constraint $|R_i| = 1$ back for one cell at a time turns the cell problem into the minimization of a quadratic on the unit sphere, whose stationarity condition is the linearized cell equation with its Lagrange multiplier left free (Section~\ref{section:exact_cell}). The linearization amounts to freezing that multiplier, and we show that the frozen value is a lower bound of the true one, with a gap made of the cell's distortion and of the disagreement of its neighbors' rotations; we solve the exact cell with Newton's method on the secular equation, with a start that makes it converge monotonically. The exact cells confirm that the linearization is accurate, and they reveal why large twists converge slowly: when a handle is turned by $180^\circ$, its rotation is orthogonal to those of its neighbors, and the rule that smooths the handle cells keeps them behind. A simple adaptive rule removes that delay without changing the result (Section~\ref{section:hard_case}).


\section{Related Work}

Since the focus of this paper is As-Rigid-As-Possible (ARAP) surface deformation, we only review previous work on ARAP deformation. ARAP surface modeling was introduced by Sorkine and Alexa \cite{Sorkine2007} to produce robust and physically plausible deformations by minimizing the deviation from rigidity of the local transformation associated with every vertex. Although results on low-resolution meshes look close to physical deformations, the method does not achieve satisfactory results on high-resolution meshes. The reason is that the ARAP energy is minimized by preserving rigidity on large parts of the mesh at the expense of concentrating all the distortion on small parts of it. This problem was solved by the Smooth Rotation enhanced ARAP method (SR-ARAP) of Levi and Gotsman \cite{Levi2015}, in which the deformation energy is extended with a term that penalizes differing rotations of nearby vertices, significantly increasing the quality of the final result. Chao \emph{et al.} \cite{Chao2010} derive the ARAP energy from the elastic energy of continuum mechanics, showing a consistent discretization for volumetric ARAP with tetrahedral cells and for 2D ARAP with triangular cells. However, the ARAP energy does not have a consistent discretization for triangulated surfaces in 3D. SR-ARAP \cite{Levi2015} also addresses this issue, providing an energy with a consistent discretization for surfaces in 3D. Further enhancements to SR-ARAP have addressed performance on large meshes \cite{Morsucci2018, Ligare2020}, made the physical stiffness adjustable \cite{Chen2017, LeVaou2020}, and introduced local scaling to the rotations, turning rigid transformations into similarity transformations \cite{Jiang2017}.



\section{As-Rigid-As-Possible Shape Deformation with Smooth Rotations}
\label{section:sr_arap}

Let $Q$ be the rest mesh, with vertices $q_1, \dots, q_m$, and $P$ the deformed mesh, with vertices $p_1, \dots, p_m$ and the same connectivity. Denote by $N(i)$ the set of $1$-ring neighbors of vertex $i$, by $d_i = |N(i)|$ its degree (valence), and by $q_{ij} = q_j - q_i$ and $p_{ij} = p_j - p_i$ the directed edges. The discrete As-Rigid-As-Possible energy with Smooth Rotations (SR-ARAP) is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sr_arap}
E(P, R) = \sum_{i=1}^m \Big( \sum_{j \in N(i)} c_{ij} \, \| R_i \, q_{ij} \, \tilde R_i - p_{ij} \|^2
+ \alpha A \sum_{j \in N(i)} w_{ij} \, \| R_j - R_i \|^2 \Big),
\end{equation}
where $R_1, \dots, R_m \in \mathbb{H}$ are unit quaternions, $\tilde R_i$ is the conjugate of $R_i$, and edge vectors are identified with pure quaternions, so that $R_i \, q_{ij} \, \tilde R_i$ is the rest edge $q_{ij}$ rotated by $R_i$.
The membrane weights $c_{ij} = \max\big(0, \tfrac{1}{2}(\cot \gamma_{ij} + \cot \delta_{ij})\big)$ are the familiar cotangent weights, where $\gamma_{ij}$ and $\delta_{ij}$ are the angles opposite the edge $ij$. They are symmetric, $c_{ij} = c_{ji}$, and non-negative, but not normalized.
The bending weights $w_{ij}$ are positive and normalized per vertex, $\sum_{j \in N(i)} w_{ij} = 1$; in this paper $w_{ij} = 1/d_i$. Since $d_i$ varies from vertex to vertex, they are \emph{not} symmetric in general: $w_{ij} \neq w_{ji}$.
$A$ is the mesh area, which makes the energy scale invariant, and $\alpha$ is a positive scalar parameter.

The main idea of the method is to break the surface into overlapping cells and keep the transformation of each cell as rigid as possible in the least-squares sense. The cells must overlap to avoid stretching or shearing the surface at cell boundaries.
The first term is a membrane energy, which penalizes stretching and shearing of a cell; the second term is a bending energy, which penalizes the difference between a cell's rotation and the rotations of its neighboring cells.
The membrane term lowers the distortion of a cell by keeping the differential of the map close to rigid.
The bending term keeps the variation of the rotations within a cell's neighborhood low, so that the neighborhood transforms as a unit as much as possible.

The vertices of $Q$ are in their original positions, the vertices of $P$ are the deformed positions, and the quaternion $R_i$ is the best rigid transformation, in the least-squares sense, relating the original and the deformed cell.
This is a non-linear optimization problem, typically solved by an iterative local/global method that solves two linear sub-problems in each iteration.
The first, the \emph{local step}, keeps the vertices of $P$ constant and finds the best rotation $R_i$ for each cell. The second, the \emph{global step}, keeps the rotations $R_i$ constant and computes the optimal deformed vertices $p_i$ in the least-squares sense. Each step can only lower the energy, so the iterations decrease it monotonically.

Both steps will be treated with the same four moves, which we spell out once because they recur throughout the paper: (1)~write the energy as a function of the unknowns of the step only; (2)~set its gradient to zero, which gives a linear system; (3)~substitute the values that are fixed by the user's constraints, which leaves a square system in the free unknowns; and (4)~show that this system is symmetric positive definite, so that it has a unique solution and can be solved with a Cholesky factorization or by conjugate gradients. Section~\ref{section:global_step} applies them to the positions, where they are classical. Section~\ref{section:local_step} applies them to the rotations, where the second and fourth moves need more care, and then shows how the resulting system is solved in near-linear time.

\section{Global Step}
\label{section:global_step}

The global step computes the optimal vertices $p_1, \dots, p_m$ for fixed rotations:
\begin{equation*}
\min_{p_1, \dots, p_m \in \mathbb R^3} \; \sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j \in N(i)} c_{ij} \, \| R_i \, q_{ij} \, \tilde R_i - p_{ij} \|^2 .
\end{equation*}
The bending term of (\ref{eq:sr_arap}) does not depend on the positions, so it is omitted. To shorten the notation, write $t^i_{ij} = R_i \, q_{ij} \, \tilde R_i$ for the rest edge $q_{ij}$ rotated by the rotation of cell $i$; it is a known vector in this step.

\paragraph{Where $p_i$ appears.} A vertex $p_i$ enters the energy in two kinds of terms: in its own cell, through every edge $p_{ij} = p_j - p_i$ with $j \in N(i)$, and in the cell of each neighbor $j$, through the edge $p_{ji} = p_i - p_j$. Differentiating one term of each kind,
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial}{\partial p_i} \| t^i_{ij} - (p_j - p_i) \|^2 &= 2 \, \big( t^i_{ij} - p_{ij} \big),
\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial p_i} \| t^j_{ji} - (p_i - p_j) \|^2 &= -2 \, \big( t^j_{ji} - p_{ji} \big) = 2 \, \big( t^j_{ij} - p_{ij} \big),
\end{align*}
where the last equality uses $q_{ji} = -q_{ij}$, hence $t^j_{ji} = -t^j_{ij}$, and $p_{ji} = -p_{ij}$. Both kinds of terms carry the same weight, since $c_{ij} = c_{ji}$.

\paragraph{Stationarity.} Summing over the neighbors and setting the gradient to zero gives $\sum_{j} c_{ij} \, ( t^i_{ij} + t^j_{ij} - 2 \, p_{ij} ) = 0$, that is, the linear system \cite{Lopez2013}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ls_for_p_ga}
\sum_{j \in N(i)} c_{ij} \, (p_j - p_i) = \sum_{j \in N(i)} \frac{c_{ij}}{2} \, \big( R_i \, q_{ij} \, \tilde R_i + R_j \, q_{ij} \, \tilde R_j \big).
\end{equation}
Each edge of the deformed mesh is asked to match the average of the rest edge rotated by the rotations of its two end cells. In matrix form the system reads $\mathrm L \, \mathrm P = \mathrm B$. Here $\mathrm L$ is the $m \times m$ discrete Laplace--Beltrami operator, $(\mathrm L \, \mathrm P)_i = \sum_{j \in N(i)} c_{ij} (p_j - p_i)$, $\mathrm P$ is the $m \times 3$ matrix of target positions, and $\mathrm B$ is the $m \times 3$ matrix whose $i$th row is the right-hand side of Equation~(\ref{eq:ls_for_p_ga}). The operator $\mathrm L$ is symmetric and negative semi-definite, since
\begin{equation*}
-x^T \mathrm L \, x = \sum_{\{i,j\} \in \mathcal E} c_{ij} \, (x_i - x_j)^2 \geq 0 \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathbb R^m,
\end{equation*}
where $\mathcal E$ is the set of mesh edges, each counted once.

\subsection{Positional constraints}
Constraints of the form $p_i = p^{const}_i$ for $i$ in a set $\mathcal C$ of constrained vertices are incorporated by substituting the corresponding variables. Let $\mathcal F$ be the remaining (free) vertices. The equations of the constrained vertices are dropped, since their positions are not unknowns any more. Partitioning $\mathrm L$, $\mathrm P$ and $\mathrm B$ accordingly, the rows of $\mathcal F$ read $\mathrm L_{\mathcal F \mathcal F} \mathrm P_{\mathcal F} + \mathrm L_{\mathcal F \mathcal C} \mathrm P_{\mathcal C} = \mathrm B_{\mathcal F}$. Moving the known part to the right and changing signs, so that the matrix is positive rather than negative,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:global_reduced}
(-\mathrm L_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}) \, \mathrm P_{\mathcal F} = -\mathrm B_{\mathcal F} + \mathrm L_{\mathcal F \mathcal C} \, \mathrm P^{const}_{\mathcal C}.
\end{equation}
This system is square, with one equation per free unknown, so no least-squares formulation (normal equations $\mathrm L^T \mathrm L$) is needed; forming the normal equations would square the condition number and increase the fill-in of the factorization.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:laplacian_spd}
If every connected component of the mesh, connected through edges with $c_{ij} > 0$, contains a constrained vertex, then $-\mathrm L_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}$ is symmetric positive definite.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
$-\mathrm L_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}$ is a principal submatrix of the symmetric matrix $-\mathrm L$, so it is symmetric. For $x \in \mathbb R^{|\mathcal F|}$, extend it by zero on $\mathcal C$; then $x^T (-\mathrm L_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}) x = \sum_{\{i,j\} \in \mathcal E} c_{ij} (x_i - x_j)^2 \geq 0$. Equality holds only if $x$ is constant on every component, and since every component contains a constrained vertex, where the extended $x$ is zero, this forces $x = 0$.
\end{proof}

Equation~(\ref{eq:global_reduced}) is therefore solved with a sparse Cholesky factorization of $-\mathrm L_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}$. This matrix depends only on the rest mesh and the constraint set, not on the rotations, so it is factored once; every global step then costs two sparse triangular solves per coordinate, and the three coordinates share the factorization. The local step will not be this fortunate: its matrix changes with the positions, and Section~\ref{section:pcg} shows how to recover a factor-once method for it.

\section{Local Step}
\label{section:local_step}

In the local step the positions are fixed and the rotations are the unknowns. Unlike the positions, the rotations cannot be found one cell at a time: the bending term couples the rotation of every cell to those of its neighbors, and through them to the whole mesh. SR-ARAP handles this coupling by relaxation. It repeatedly visits the cells and finds the best rotation of each one with its neighbors' rotations held fixed, and since only two or three such sweeps can be afforded per iteration, the rotations it returns are approximate.

We follow the four moves of Section~\ref{section:sr_arap} instead. First we make the energy quadratic by treating quaternions as vectors of $\mathbb R^4$ (Section~\ref{section:linearization}). We then write the equation of a single cell, which is the equation relaxation solves (Section~\ref{section:one_cell}), and require it to hold at every cell at once, which gives one linear system for all the rotations (Section~\ref{section:all_cells}). After a scaling of its rows the system is symmetric, and we identify the energy it minimizes (Section~\ref{section:exact_energy}). Substituting the constrained rotations, we prove that it is positive definite (Section~\ref{section:rotation_constraints}), discuss the role of the feedback term (Section~\ref{section:feedback}), and finally show how it is solved in near-linear time (Section~\ref{section:pcg}).

\subsection{Rotations as vectors of $\mathbb R^4$}
\label{section:linearization}

Three observations turn the membrane term of a cell into a quadratic form in the coefficients of its quaternion.

\emph{(i) The conjugate can be removed.} Multiplying a quaternion by a unit quaternion does not change its norm. Multiplying $R_i \, q_{ij} \, \tilde R_i - p_{ij}$ on the right by $R_i$ and using $\tilde R_i R_i = 1$,
\begin{equation*}
\| R_i \, q_{ij} \, \tilde R_i - p_{ij} \| = \| (R_i \, q_{ij} \, \tilde R_i - p_{ij}) \, R_i \| = \| R_i \, q_{ij} - p_{ij} \, R_i \| .
\end{equation*}

\emph{(ii) The result is linear in $R_i$.} Both $R_i \, q_{ij}$ and $p_{ij} \, R_i$ are linear in the four coefficients of $R_i$, so $R_i \, q_{ij} - p_{ij} \, R_i = M_{ij} R_i$ for a $4 \times 4$ real matrix $M_{ij}$ that depends only on $q_{ij}$ and $p_{ij}$. Section~\ref{section:the_form_of_arap_matrix} gives it explicitly.

\emph{(iii) The membrane term is a quadratic form.} Since $\| M_{ij} R_i \|^2 = R_i^T M_{ij}^T M_{ij} R_i$, summing over the cell gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:membrane_quadratic}
\sum_{j \in N(i)} c_{ij} \, \| R_i \, q_{ij} - p_{ij} \, R_i \|^2 = R_i^T M_i \, R_i, \qquad M_i = \sum_{j \in N(i)} c_{ij} \, M_{ij}^T M_{ij}.
\end{equation}
Each $M_{ij}^T M_{ij}$ is symmetric positive semi-definite and the weights $c_{ij}$ are non-negative, so $M_i$ is a symmetric positive semi-definite $4 \times 4$ matrix.

The bending term needs no work: $\| R_j - R_i \|^2$ is already the squared Euclidean distance of $\mathbb R^4$.

\paragraph{Linearization.} Observation~(i) holds only for $|R_i| = 1$. We now drop this constraint and treat the $R_i$ as arbitrary vectors of $\mathbb R^4$. All energies of the local step then become quadratic, and their stationarity conditions linear; the quaternions are normalized after the solve. As shown in \cite{Ligare2020}, this linearization gives an accurate approximation of the optimal quaternion in the least-squares sense, and our results confirm that it does not affect the accuracy of SR-ARAP rotations in any significant way. Two things deserve a comment. First, without the unit norm nothing in the membrane term prevents the trivial answer $R_i = 0$. What keeps the rotations away from zero are the known rotations that will appear on the right-hand side of the equations: those of the constrained cells (Section~\ref{section:rotation_constraints}) and the previous rotations of the feedback term (Section~\ref{section:one_cell}). Second, $R$ and $-R$ represent the same rotation but are far apart in $\mathbb R^4$, so the bending term is meaningful only if neighboring quaternions lie in the same hemisphere; starting every local step from the previous rotations keeps them there.

The linearized SR-ARAP energy of the local step is then, with the weights $w_{ij} = 1/d_i$ of Section~\ref{section:sr_arap},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:local_energy}
E(R) = \sum_{i=1}^m \Big( R_i^T M_i \, R_i + \alpha A \sum_{j \in N(i)} w_{ij} \, \| R_j - R_i \|^2 \Big).
\end{equation}

\subsection{The equation of one cell}
\label{section:one_cell}

We start with the simplest problem: the best rotation of a single cell $i$ when the rotations $R_j$ of its neighbors are known. It is the problem relaxation solves for one cell at a time, and its equation is the building block of everything that follows.

\paragraph{Feedback.} Before writing the equation we add one term to the energy of the cell. During interactive editing only a few local/global iterations run per frame, and the rotations of one frame should stay close to those of the previous frame; otherwise they can jump between frames and, for large rotations, even reverse their sense of rotation, which shows as artifacts in the animation. We therefore let the previous rotation $R^{prev}_i$ of the cell act as one more neighbor, with weight $e > 0$ where each true neighbor has weight $1$. The energy of the cell becomes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cell_energy}
\begin{gathered}
E_i(R_i) = R_i^T M_i \, R_i + \alpha A \Big( \sum_{j \in N(i)} w_{ij} \, \| R_i - R_j \|^2 + \epsilon_i \, \| R_i - R^{prev}_i \|^2 \Big),
\\
w_{ij} = \frac{1}{d_i + e}, \quad \epsilon_i = \frac{e}{d_i + e}.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The bending weights are renormalized to make room for the new neighbor: they still sum to one, $\sum_{j \in N(i)} w_{ij} + \epsilon_i = 1$, like those of SR-ARAP, and for $e = 0$ they are exactly SR-ARAP's, $w_{ij} = 1/d_i$. From now on $w_{ij}$ denotes these renormalized weights. Section~\ref{section:feedback} discusses what the feedback brings and what it costs.

\paragraph{The gradient.} Two rules suffice: for a symmetric matrix $M$, the gradient of $R^T M R$ is $2 M R$; and for a fixed vector $X$, the gradient of $\| R - X \|^2$ is $2 (R - X)$. Applying them term by term,
\begin{equation*}
\tfrac{1}{2} \, \frac{\partial E_i}{\partial R_i} = M_i \, R_i + \alpha A \sum_{j \in N(i)} w_{ij} \, (R_i - R_j) + \alpha A \, \epsilon_i \, (R_i - R^{prev}_i).
\end{equation*}
The terms that contain $R_i$ add up to $\big( M_i + \alpha A \, (\sum_j w_{ij} + \epsilon_i) \, I \big) R_i = (M_i + \alpha A \, I) \, R_i$, thanks to the normalization of the weights.

\paragraph{The cell equation.} Setting the gradient to zero, the best rotation of cell $i$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{eqn:local_solution_feedback}
\begin{aligned}
(M_i + \alpha A \, I) \, R_i &= \alpha A \sum_{j \in N(i)} w_{ij} \, R_j + \alpha A \, \epsilon_i \, R^{prev}_i \\
&= \frac{\alpha A}{d_i + e} \Big( \sum_{j \in N(i)} R_j + e \, R^{prev}_i \Big).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The right-hand side is a weighted average of the neighbors' rotations and the previous rotation, scaled by $\alpha A$; the matrix on the left adds the membrane term of the cell, which pulls the average towards the rotation that best fits the cell's own edges. Since $M_i \succeq 0$, the matrix $M_i + \alpha A \, I \succeq \alpha A \, I$ is positive definite, so for given neighbors (\ref{eqn:local_solution_feedback}) has a unique solution, found with one $4 \times 4$ linear solve. Relaxation sweeps through the cells and solves (\ref{eqn:local_solution_feedback}) for each one with the current rotations of its neighbors: it is a block Gauss--Seidel iteration, and after a few sweeps its rotations satisfy (\ref{eqn:local_solution_feedback}) only approximately.

\subsection{All cells at once}
\label{section:all_cells}

Instead of solving the cell equations one at a time, we now ask for rotations that satisfy (\ref{eqn:local_solution_feedback}) at every cell simultaneously; these are the rotations relaxation converges to. Every cell equation is linear in the rotations, so together they form one linear system, with four equations and four unknowns per cell. For the moment we treat every cell as unknown; the constrained cells are handled in Section~\ref{section:rotation_constraints}.

\paragraph{The system is not symmetric.} Moving the neighbors to the left, the equations of cell $i$ read
\begin{equation*}
(M_i + \alpha A \, I) \, R_i - \frac{\alpha A}{d_i + e} \sum_{j \in N(i)} R_j = \alpha A \, \epsilon_i \, R^{prev}_i .
\end{equation*}
The coefficient of $R_j$ in the equations of cell $i$ is $-\frac{\alpha A}{d_i + e} \, I$, while the coefficient of $R_i$ in the equations of cell $j$ is $-\frac{\alpha A}{d_j + e} \, I$. The two differ whenever $d_i \neq d_j$, which happens on every mesh that is not perfectly regular, so the system is not symmetric.

\paragraph{Why symmetry matters.} A symmetric positive definite system can be solved with a Cholesky factorization, which needs no pivoting and half the work of an LU factorization, or by conjugate gradients, which only need products with the matrix. Moreover, a symmetric system is the stationarity condition of an energy (Section~\ref{section:exact_energy}), which tells us what its solution minimizes. None of this is available for a non-symmetric system.

\paragraph{Restoring symmetry.} Multiplying the equations of a cell by a nonzero number does not change the solution. Multiplying those of cell $i$ by
\begin{equation*}
s_i = \frac{d_i + e}{\alpha A}
\end{equation*}
turns every coupling coefficient into $-I$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eqn:local_symmetric}
s_i \, (M_i + \alpha A \, I) \, R_i - \sum_{j \in N(i)} R_j = e \, R^{prev}_i .
\end{equation}
In matrix form this is $\mathrm K \, \mathrm R = e \, \mathrm R^{prev}$, where $\mathrm R$ and $\mathrm R^{prev}$ are the $4m \times 1$ column vectors stacking the coefficients of the quaternions $R_i$ and $R^{prev}_i$, and $\mathrm K$ is the $4m \times 4m$ matrix with $4 \times 4$ blocks
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm K_{ii} = s_i \, (M_i + \alpha A \, I), \qquad
\mathrm K_{ij} = -I \ \text{if} \ \{i, j\} \in \mathcal E, \qquad
\mathrm K_{ij} = 0 \ \text{otherwise}.
\end{equation*}
$\mathrm K$ is symmetric, because the adjacency of the mesh is.

\subsection{The energy minimized by the system}
\label{section:exact_energy}

A symmetric linear system always comes from an energy. If $\mathrm K$ is symmetric, the gradient of $f(x) = \frac{1}{2} x^T \mathrm K x - b^T x$ is $\mathrm K x - b$, so $\mathrm K x = b$ states that $x$ is a stationary point of $f$; if $\mathrm K$ is moreover positive definite, $f$ is strictly convex and its stationary point is its unique minimizer. The following proposition writes this energy for the rotation system in terms of the mesh.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:exact_energy}
The system $\mathrm K \, \mathrm R = e \, \mathrm R^{prev}$ is the exact stationarity condition of the strictly convex quadratic energy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:exact_energy}
\tilde E(R) = \sum_{i=1}^m (d_i + e) \, R_i^T M_i \, R_i
+ \alpha A \sum_{\{i,j\} \in \mathcal E} \| R_i - R_j \|^2
+ \alpha A \, e \sum_{i=1}^m \| R_i - R^{prev}_i \|^2 ,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal E$ is the set of mesh edges, each counted once. Its unique minimizer is therefore the set of rotations that satisfies the cell equation (\ref{eqn:local_solution_feedback}) at every cell simultaneously.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
We compute the gradient of $\tilde E$ with respect to $R_i$ term by term. The membrane term contributes $2 (d_i + e) M_i R_i$. The edge $\{i, j\}$ appears once in the bending sum and contributes $2 \alpha A (R_i - R_j)$; vertex $i$ has $d_i$ such edges. The feedback term contributes $2 \alpha A e (R_i - R^{prev}_i)$. Hence
\begin{align*}
\tfrac{1}{2} \, \frac{\partial \tilde E}{\partial R_i}
&= (d_i + e) \, M_i \, R_i + \alpha A \sum_{j \in N(i)} (R_i - R_j) + \alpha A \, e \, (R_i - R^{prev}_i) \\
&= (d_i + e) \, M_i \, R_i + \alpha A \, (d_i + e) \, R_i - \alpha A \sum_{j \in N(i)} R_j - \alpha A \, e \, R^{prev}_i \\
&= \alpha A \Big( s_i \, (M_i + \alpha A \, I) \, R_i - \sum_{j \in N(i)} R_j - e \, R^{prev}_i \Big),
\end{align*}
which vanishes exactly when (\ref{eqn:local_symmetric}) holds. The first two terms of $\tilde E$ are positive semi-definite quadratic forms and the feedback term is positive definite, so $\tilde E$ is strictly convex and its stationary point is its unique minimizer.
\end{proof}

$\tilde E$ is the linearized SR-ARAP energy (\ref{eq:local_energy}) with three differences: the membrane term of cell $i$ is weighted by $d_i + e$, the bending term is the symmetric, unweighted sum over edges, and the feedback appears as an explicit proximal term towards the previous rotations. Solving $\mathrm K \, \mathrm R = e \, \mathrm R^{prev}$ is thus not an approximation of relaxation: it gives in one step the exact minimizer of $\tilde E$, which relaxation can only approach iteratively. We call this solution the \emph{closed form} of the local step.

\begin{rem}
\label{rem:true_gradient}
Why does the system not minimize the sum of the cell energies, (\ref{eq:local_energy})? Each cell equation minimizes $E_i$ over $R_i$ with the neighbors held fixed, but in the total energy $R_i$ also appears in the bending terms of its neighbors' cells, so
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:true_gradient}
\tfrac{1}{2} \, \frac{\partial E}{\partial R_i} = M_i \, R_i + \alpha A \sum_{j \in N(i)} (w_{ij} + w_{ji}) \, (R_i - R_j).
\end{equation}
The cell equations ignore the second weight, $w_{ji}$, and the two conditions coincide only when $w_{ij} = w_{ji}$, which per-vertex normalized weights do not satisfy. Minimizing $E$ itself would lead to another symmetric system, with the weights $w_{ij} + w_{ji}$. We keep (\ref{eqn:local_symmetric}) because its equations are the cell equations themselves: every rotation is the best one for its own cell given its neighbors, as in the cell-by-cell formulation, and relaxation of (\ref{eqn:local_solution_feedback}) converges to the same solution (Section~\ref{section:rotation_constraints}), which lets us measure the error of relaxation exactly (Section~\ref{section:results}).
\end{rem}

\subsection{Constrained rotations and positive definiteness}
\label{section:rotation_constraints}

The vertices inside the handles are constrained, $i \in \mathcal C$. Their positions are prescribed, and their rotations are not unknowns of the local step either: each is computed directly from the cell equation without feedback ($e = 0$), with the neighboring rotations $R^{prev}_j$ of the previous iteration,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rotation_constraint}
R^{const}_i = (M_i + \alpha A \, I)^{-1} \, \frac{\alpha A}{d_i} \sum_{j \in N(i)} R^{prev}_j , \qquad i \in \mathcal C,
\end{equation}
and normalized. Each constrained rotation therefore costs one $4 \times 4$ linear solve, which is always possible since $M_i + \alpha A \, I$ is positive definite. This rule has one blind spot: when a handle is turned by a large angle at once, (\ref{eq:rotation_constraint}) detaches the cells of the handle from the handle. Section~\ref{section:hard_case} explains why and replaces (\ref{eq:rotation_constraint}), in that situation only, by the cell's own fit; the resulting \emph{adaptive rule} (\ref{eq:own_fit}) is the one our method uses. For what follows it only matters that every $R^{const}_i$ is known before the rotation system is solved.

\paragraph{The reduced system.} As in the global step, we keep only the equations of the free cells $\mathcal F$ and move the known rotations to the right: $\mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F} \, \mathrm R_{\mathcal F} = e \, \mathrm R^{prev}_{\mathcal F} - \mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal C} \, \mathrm R^{const}_{\mathcal C}$. Since the coupling blocks of $\mathrm K$ are $-I$, the four equations of a free cell $i$ read
\begin{equation}
\label{eqn:local_reduced}
s_i \, (M_i + \alpha A \, I) \, R_i - \sum_{j \in N(i) \cap \mathcal F} R_j = e \, R^{prev}_i + \sum_{j \in N(i) \cap \mathcal C} R^{const}_j , \qquad i \in \mathcal F:
\end{equation}
a constrained neighbor simply adds its rotation to the right-hand side. Equivalently, $\mathrm R_{\mathcal F}$ minimizes $\tilde E$ over the free rotations, with the constrained ones fixed to $R^{const}_i$.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:rotation_spd}
$\mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}$ is symmetric positive definite, with smallest eigenvalue at least $e$.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
It is a principal submatrix of the symmetric matrix $\mathrm K$, so it is symmetric. Since $M_i$ is positive semi-definite, the diagonal blocks satisfy $s_i (M_i + \alpha A \, I) \succeq s_i \, \alpha A \, I = (d_i + e) \, I$. For $x \in \mathbb R^{4 |\mathcal F|}$ with blocks $x_i$, each edge between two free vertices contributes $-2 \, x_i \cdot x_j \geq -\|x_i\|^2 - \|x_j\|^2$ to $x^T \mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F} \, x$, so vertex $i$ loses at most $\|x_i\|^2$ per free neighbor, and it has at most $d_i$ of them:
\begin{equation*}
x^T \mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F} \, x \geq \sum_{i \in \mathcal F} (d_i + e) \, \|x_i\|^2 - \sum_{i \in \mathcal F} |N(i) \cap \mathcal F| \, \|x_i\|^2 \geq e \, \|x\|^2 . \qedhere
\end{equation*}
\end{proof}

Three consequences follow. First, unlike Proposition~\ref{prop:laplacian_spd}, the result holds for any constraint set, even an empty one: the feedback term alone makes the system positive definite. Second, relaxation converges: it is block Gauss--Seidel on (\ref{eqn:local_reduced}), and Gauss--Seidel converges from any starting point for every symmetric positive definite matrix, a classical result of numerical linear algebra, so the closed form and relaxation share the same solution. Third, (\ref{eqn:local_reduced}) can be solved with a sparse Cholesky factorization, with no least-squares formulation. Its sparsity pattern is that of the mesh graph with $4 \times 4$ blocks and depends only on the mesh and the constraint set, so the symbolic analysis (the fill-reducing ordering) is computed once. The values, however, change in every iteration, since $M_i$ depends on the deformed positions, so a direct solve costs one numeric factorization per local step, whose cost grows as $n^{1.5}$ on surface meshes. Section~\ref{section:pcg} removes that factorization. In either case the quaternions $R_i$ are normalized after the solve.

\subsection{The role of the feedback}
\label{section:feedback}

With the system and its properties in hand, we can now say precisely what the feedback term of Section~\ref{section:one_cell} contributes. It has four benefits. (i)~\emph{Temporal coherence}: during interactive editing only a few iterations run per frame, and the feedback keeps every local step close to the previous one, so rotations do not pop between frames. (ii)~\emph{Large rotations}: near and beyond $180^\circ$ the linearized fit has two nearly equivalent senses of rotation; anchoring each cell to its previous rotation favors the branch it was following, which prevents abrupt changes in the sense of rotation and lets a large twist spread evenly along the surface as it grows (Section~\ref{section:results_twist}). (iii)~\emph{Conditioning}: it bounds the smallest eigenvalue of the rotation system by $e$ for any mesh and any constraint set (Proposition~\ref{prop:rotation_spd}), so the system stays solvable even without constraints, and iterative solvers of it converge in a number of iterations independent of the mesh (Section~\ref{section:results}). (iv)~\emph{Little bias}: it barely changes the converged shape (Remark~\ref{rem:feedback_bias}). Its cost is a damping of the outer local/global loop, which needs more iterations to reach its fixed point when the rotations are large.

\begin{rem}
\label{rem:feedback_bias}
How much does the feedback change the answer? At a fixed point of the local/global loop the rotations no longer change, $R_i = R^{prev}_i$, so (\ref{eqn:local_solution_feedback}) becomes $(M_i + \alpha A \, I) R_i = \frac{\alpha A}{d_i + e} \big( \sum_{j} R_j + e R_i \big)$. Moving the term $e R_i$ to the left gives $M_i R_i + \frac{\alpha A \, d_i}{d_i + e} R_i = \frac{\alpha A}{d_i + e} \sum_j R_j$, and multiplying by $(d_i + e)/d_i$,
\begin{equation*}
\Big( \frac{d_i + e}{d_i} \, M_i + \alpha A \, I \Big) R_i = \frac{\alpha A}{d_i} \sum_{j \in N(i)} R_j .
\end{equation*}
This is the cell equation without feedback, (\ref{eqn:local_solution_feedback}) with $e = 0$, except that the membrane term of each cell is weighted by $(d_i + e)/d_i$, about $1.03$ for $e = 0.2$ and a typical valence of 6. The feedback therefore acts as a proximal damping of the iterations rather than as a change of the model. On the bar bent into a $90^\circ$ arc, the shapes converged with $e = 0.2$ and with $e = 0$ (3{,}000 iterations each) differ by at most $0.33\%$ of the bar's length, and their ARAP distortions by $0.9\%$.
\end{rem}

\subsection{Solving the rotation system in near-linear time}
\label{section:pcg}

\paragraph{The cost of a direct solve.} A sparse Cholesky factorization of $\mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}$ creates fill-in: with the best orderings, nested dissection, a surface mesh of $n$ vertices produces $O(n \log n)$ nonzeros in the factor and costs $O(n^{1.5})$ operations to factor. The global step pays this price once, because its matrix does not change. The rotation system pays it in every local step, because its diagonal blocks contain the matrices $M_i$, which depend on the deformed positions. The remedy is to separate the part of $\mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}$ that changes from the part that does not.

\paragraph{Splitting the matrix.} Write
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:K_split}
\mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F} = \mathrm C \otimes I_4 + \mathrm D,
\qquad
\mathrm D = \mathrm{diag}\big( s_i M_i \big)_{i \in \mathcal F},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm C$ is the $|\mathcal F| \times |\mathcal F|$ scalar matrix with diagonal $s_i (\alpha A + \eta)$ and the entry $-1$ for every edge between free vertices, and $\eta$ is the small diagonal regularization of Section~\ref{section:implementation}. The Kronecker product $\mathrm C \otimes I_4$ is the matrix that applies $\mathrm C$ to each of the four quaternion components separately: arranging the rotations as an $|\mathcal F| \times 4$ matrix, one column per component, it multiplies that matrix by $\mathrm C$. $\mathrm C$ depends only on the mesh, the constraint set and the feedback weight. $\mathrm D$ is block diagonal and positive semi-definite, and carries all the dependence on the deformed positions.

\paragraph{Conjugate gradients.} For a symmetric positive definite system, the conjugate gradient method needs only products with the matrix, and its error, measured in the energy norm, decreases at least by the factor $(\sqrt{\kappa} - 1)/(\sqrt{\kappa} + 1)$ per iteration, where $\kappa$ is the condition number of the matrix. The preconditioned method (PCG) uses a second symmetric positive definite matrix $\mathrm P$, the preconditioner, and behaves like conjugate gradients on $\mathrm P^{-1} \mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}$; each iteration then also solves one system with $\mathrm P$. A good preconditioner is close to the matrix, so that $\kappa(\mathrm P^{-1} \mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F})$ is small, and cheap to solve with. The constant part $\mathrm P = \mathrm C \otimes I_4$ is a natural candidate on both counts: it is all of $\mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}$ except the positive semi-definite part $\mathrm D$, and since it does not change, it can be factored once, like the Laplacian of the global step. The following proposition shows how good it is.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:conditioning}
Let $\mu = \max_{i \in \mathcal F} s_i \, \lambda_{\max}(M_i)$ and $d_{\max} = \max_i d_i$. Then
\begin{equation*}
\kappa\big(\mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}\big) \leq \frac{2 d_{\max} + e + \mu}{e} \, (1 + \eta / \alpha A),
\qquad
\kappa\big((\mathrm C \otimes I_4)^{-1} \mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}\big) \leq 1 + \frac{\mu}{e}.
\end{equation*}
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
The diagonal of $\mathrm C$ is $s_i (\alpha A + \eta) = (d_i + e)(1 + \eta / \alpha A) \geq d_i + e$ and its off-diagonal entries in row $i$ sum in absolute value to at most $d_i$, so by Gershgorin's theorem the eigenvalues of $\mathrm C$, and hence those of $\mathrm C \otimes I_4$, lie in $[e, (2 d_{\max} + e)(1 + \eta / \alpha A)]$. The diagonal blocks of $\mathrm D$ are $s_i M_i$, with eigenvalues between $0$ and $\mu$, so $0 \preceq \mathrm D \preceq \mu I$. Adding the two, the eigenvalues of $\mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}$ lie in $[e, (2 d_{\max} + e)(1 + \eta / \alpha A) + \mu]$, and the ratio of the endpoints is bounded by the first expression. For the second bound, the eigenvalues of $(\mathrm C \otimes I_4)^{-1} \mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}$ are the values of the generalized Rayleigh quotient
\begin{equation*}
\frac{x^T \mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F} \, x}{x^T (\mathrm C \otimes I_4) \, x} = 1 + \frac{x^T \mathrm D \, x}{x^T (\mathrm C \otimes I_4) \, x} \in \Big[ 1, \, 1 + \frac{\mu}{e} \Big],
\end{equation*}
since $0 \leq x^T \mathrm D \, x \leq \mu \|x\|^2$ and $x^T (\mathrm C \otimes I_4) \, x \geq e \|x\|^2$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{What the bounds say.} Both bounds are independent of the resolution of the mesh. Moreover $\mu$ decreases under refinement: $\lambda_{\max}(M_i) \leq \mathrm{tr}\, M_i = 4 \sum_j c_{ij} (\|p_{ij}\|^2 + \|q_{ij}\|^2)$ (Section~\ref{section:the_form_of_arap_matrix}) scales with the squared edge length, while $s_i$ does not depend on the resolution, so for a uniform refinement of a fixed surface with bounded stretching $\mu = O(1/n)$, and the preconditioned condition number tends to 1. We therefore solve (\ref{eqn:local_reduced}) by PCG with preconditioner $\mathrm C \otimes I_4$, warm-started from the previous rotations. By the convergence rate above, the number of iterations needed for a given accuracy is bounded independently of the mesh, and decreases as it is refined.

\paragraph{Cost.} $\mathrm C$ is factored once per constraint set with a sparse Cholesky factorization, like the Poisson systems of Sections~\ref{section:global_step} and~\ref{section:harmonic_interpolation_rotors}. Every PCG iteration then costs one product with $\mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}$, which is $O(n)$, and one application of the preconditioner: two triangular solves with four right-hand sides, $O(n \log n)$ for the nested-dissection orderings of surface meshes. A local step therefore costs $O(n \log n)$, instead of the $O(n^{1.5})$ of a numeric factorization. We stop PCG at a relative residual of $10^{-12}$, where its rotations match those of the direct solve to within $10^{-12}$~rad, so the method keeps the exactness of the closed form. Without a preconditioner, conjugate gradients also converges in a mesh-independent number of iterations by the first bound of Proposition~\ref{prop:conditioning}, at $O(n)$ per iteration: a linear-time solver, at the price of more iterations.

\subsection{The linearization seen from the unit sphere}
\label{section:exact_cell}

In Section~\ref{section:linearization} we dropped the constraint $|R_i| = 1$, solved, and normalized afterwards. The results confirm that this works, but they do not say \emph{why}, nor where it might not. To find out, we now put the constraint back, for one cell at a time, and solve the cell problem exactly on the unit sphere. Comparing the exact cell with the linearized one will show precisely what the linearization changes, how large the change is, and in which situation it is not small.

\paragraph{The cell energy on the sphere.} Take the energy (\ref{eq:cell_energy}) of cell $i$ with its neighbors held fixed, and now require $|R_i| = 1$. On the sphere the bending terms simplify: for unit quaternions,
\begin{equation*}
\| R_i - R_j \|^2 = |R_i|^2 + |R_j|^2 - 2 \, R_i \cdot R_j = 2 - 2 \, R_i \cdot R_j ,
\end{equation*}
so each of them is a constant minus a term that is \emph{linear} in $R_i$. Collecting the linear terms, and recalling that the weights $w_{ij}$ and $\epsilon_i$ sum to one, the cell energy becomes, up to a constant,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sphere_energy}
\begin{gathered}
E_i(R_i) = R_i^T M_i \, R_i - 2 \, h_i^T R_i ,
\\
h_i = \alpha A \, g_i, \qquad g_i = \sum_{j \in N(i)} w_{ij} \, R_j + \epsilon_i \, R^{prev}_i .
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The vector $g_i$ is a weighted average of unit quaternions with weights that sum to one: the \emph{mean rotation} of the cell's neighborhood, which is a unit vector only when all the neighbors agree. The vector $h_i$, the \emph{pull} of the neighbors, is that mean scaled by the stiffness $\alpha A$. On the sphere, the cell looks for the rotation that fits its own edges, the quadratic term, and that points along the pull, the linear term.

\paragraph{The stationarity condition.} To minimize (\ref{eq:sphere_energy}) on the sphere we use a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ for the constraint $|R_i|^2 = 1$. The gradient of $E_i - \lambda (|R_i|^2 - 1)$ vanishes when
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:secular_system}
(M_i - \lambda I) \, R_i = h_i , \qquad |R_i| = 1 .
\end{equation}
Geometrically, $\lambda$ measures the part of the energy gradient that is normal to the sphere: at the solution, $\frac12 \nabla E_i = M_i R_i - h_i = \lambda R_i$ points along $R_i$, so it cannot be lowered by moving on the sphere. Now compare (\ref{eq:secular_system}) with the linearized cell equation (\ref{eqn:local_solution_feedback}), $(M_i + \alpha A \, I) R_i = h_i$. They are the same equation, with the multiplier \emph{frozen} at $\lambda = -\alpha A$. This is what the linearization does, and all it does: instead of choosing $\lambda$ so that the solution has unit length, it uses a fixed value and normalizes afterwards.

\paragraph{How far is the frozen value from the true one?} Multiplying (\ref{eq:secular_system}) by $R_i^T$ and using $|R_i| = 1$ gives the multiplier of a solution $R^*_i$ explicitly, $\lambda^* = R_i^{*T} M_i R^*_i - h_i \cdot R^*_i$. The two terms can be read off the mesh:
\begin{prop}
\label{prop:multiplier}
The multiplier of a solution of (\ref{eq:secular_system}) satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:multiplier_gap}
\begin{aligned}
\lambda^* + \alpha A = {} & R_i^{*T} M_i \, R^*_i + \frac{\alpha A}{2} \, |g_i - R^*_i|^2 \\
& + \frac{\alpha A}{2} \Big( \sum_{j \in N(i)} w_{ij} \, |R_j - g_i|^2 + \epsilon_i \, |R^{prev}_i - g_i|^2 \Big),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
a sum of three non-negative terms. In particular $\lambda^* \geq -\alpha A$: the frozen multiplier is a lower bound of the true one.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
From $\lambda^* = R_i^{*T} M_i R^*_i - \alpha A \, g_i \cdot R^*_i$ we get $\lambda^* + \alpha A = R_i^{*T} M_i R^*_i + \alpha A \, (1 - g_i \cdot R^*_i)$. Since $|R^*_i| = 1$, $1 - g_i \cdot R^*_i = \frac12 |g_i - R^*_i|^2 + \frac12 (1 - |g_i|^2)$. Finally, for unit vectors $X_k$ averaged with weights $\omega_k \geq 0$ that sum to one, $\sum_k \omega_k |X_k - g|^2 = \sum_k \omega_k |X_k|^2 - |g|^2 = 1 - |g|^2$, where $g = \sum_k \omega_k X_k$; applied to the neighbors and the previous rotation, this is the last term.
\end{proof}

Each term has a plain meaning. The first is the membrane energy of the cell at its rotation: its ARAP distortion, which is zero for a cell that moves rigidly and small for one that deforms little. The second is the distance between the cell's rotation and the mean rotation of its neighborhood. The third is the spread of the neighborhood's rotations around their mean, its variance. In a smooth, nearly rigid deformation all three are small compared with $\alpha A$, so the true multiplier is close to the frozen one, and the linearized cell is close to the exact one. The proposition also says when they differ: where cells deform strongly, or where neighboring rotations disagree.

\paragraph{Which solution is the minimizer?} The system (\ref{eq:secular_system}) can have several solutions, with different multipliers, as the eigenvector equation does when $h_i = 0$. The following classical result \cite{Gander1989, MoreSorensen1983} picks the right one. Let $m_1 \leq m_2 \leq m_3 \leq m_4$ be the eigenvalues of $M_i$ and $v_1, \dots, v_4$ its unit eigenvectors.
\begin{prop}
\label{prop:sphere_minimum}
A solution $(R^*_i, \lambda^*)$ of (\ref{eq:secular_system}) with $\lambda^* \leq m_1$ is a global minimizer of (\ref{eq:sphere_energy}) on the unit sphere.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
For any unit quaternion $S$, write $G = M_i - \lambda^* I$, which is positive semi-definite since $\lambda^* \leq m_1$. Using $h_i = G R^*_i$ and $|S| = |R^*_i| = 1$,
\begin{align*}
E_i(S) - E_i(R^*_i) &= \big( S^T G S + \lambda^* - 2 R_i^{*T} G S \big) - \big( R_i^{*T} G R^*_i + \lambda^* - 2 R_i^{*T} G R^*_i \big) \\
&= (S - R^*_i)^T G \, (S - R^*_i) \geq 0 .
\end{align*}
Here we used $S^T M_i S = S^T G S + \lambda^* |S|^2$ and $h_i^T S = R_i^{*T} G S$.
\end{proof}
So we look for the multiplier below the smallest eigenvalue of $M_i$. Note that $m_1$ has a meaning of its own: its eigenvector $v_1$ is the rotation that fits the cell's edges best, the ARAP rotation of the cell alone, and $m_1$ is its residual.

\paragraph{The secular equation.} For $\lambda < m_1$ the matrix $M_i - \lambda I$ is positive definite, and (\ref{eq:secular_system}) has the unique solution $R(\lambda) = (M_i - \lambda I)^{-1} h_i$. Only its length depends on the choice of $\lambda$: writing $c_k = v_k \cdot h_i$ for the components of the pull in the eigenbasis,
\begin{equation*}
|R(\lambda)|^2 = \sum_{k=1}^4 \frac{c_k^2}{(m_k - \lambda)^2} .
\end{equation*}
As $\lambda$ decreases from $m_1$ to $-\infty$, every term decreases, from $+\infty$ (if $c_1 \neq 0$) to $0$, so exactly one $\lambda^* < m_1$ gives $|R(\lambda^*)| = 1$: the multiplier of the minimizer. Finding it is a one-dimensional root-finding problem, but $|R(\lambda)|$ has a pole at $m_1$, which makes Newton's method on $|R(\lambda)| - 1$ overshoot. The standard remedy \cite{MoreSorensen1983} is to apply Newton's method to the reciprocal, $\psi(\lambda) = 1/|R(\lambda)|$, which is almost linear.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:secular_newton}
On $(-\infty, m_1)$, $\psi$ is decreasing and concave. Newton's method for $\psi(\lambda) = 1$, started at any $\lambda^{(0)} \in [\lambda^*, m_1)$, produces a decreasing sequence that converges to $\lambda^*$.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
Let $\phi(\lambda) = |R(\lambda)|^2 = \sum_k c_k^2 t_k^{-2}$ with $t_k = m_k - \lambda > 0$, so that $\psi = \phi^{-1/2}$. Since $dt_k/d\lambda = -1$, $\phi' = 2 \sum_k c_k^2 t_k^{-3} > 0$ and $\phi'' = 6 \sum_k c_k^2 t_k^{-4}$; hence $\psi' = -\frac12 \phi^{-3/2} \phi' < 0$, and
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\psi'' &= \tfrac14 \, \phi^{-5/2} \big( 3 \phi'^2 - 2 \phi \, \phi'' \big) \\
&= 3 \, \phi^{-5/2} \Big( \big( \textstyle\sum_k c_k^2 t_k^{-3} \big)^2 - \big( \sum_k c_k^2 t_k^{-2} \big) \big( \sum_k c_k^2 t_k^{-4} \big) \Big) \leq 0
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
by the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality applied to the vectors $(|c_k| t_k^{-1})_k$ and $(|c_k| t_k^{-2})_k$. Now let $\lambda^{(t)} \in (\lambda^*, m_1)$. There $\psi < 1$, so the Newton step $-(\psi - 1)/\psi'$ is negative and $\lambda^{(t+1)} < \lambda^{(t)}$. A concave function lies below its tangents, so at $\lambda^{(t+1)}$, where the tangent equals $1$, $\psi(\lambda^{(t+1)}) \leq 1$, that is $\lambda^{(t+1)} \geq \lambda^*$. The sequence is decreasing and bounded below by $\lambda^*$, so it converges, and its limit solves $\psi = 1$.
\end{proof}

The proposition is the mirror image of the usual argument for Newton's method on a convex function, and it needs a start between the root and the pole. One is at hand. Since $|R(\lambda)| \geq |c_1| / (m_1 - \lambda)$, the point $\lambda^{(0)} = m_1 - |c_1| = m_1 - |v_1 \cdot h_i|$ has $|R| \geq 1$, so it lies in $[\lambda^*, m_1)$. Each Newton step needs $|R|$ and $\psi'$; with a Cholesky factorization $M_i - \lambda I = L L^T$, which exists because the matrix is positive definite,
\begin{gather*}
R = L^{-T} L^{-1} h_i, \qquad \psi'(\lambda) = -\frac{R^T (M_i - \lambda I)^{-1} R}{|R|^3} = -\frac{|L^{-1} R|^2}{|R|^3},
\\
\lambda \leftarrow \lambda - \frac{\psi(\lambda) - 1}{\psi'(\lambda)} = \lambda + \frac{|R|^2 \, (1 - |R|)}{|L^{-1} R|^2} .
\end{gather*}
A $4 \times 4$ factorization and three triangular solves per step: the whole cell costs a few hundred floating-point operations, and Newton's method converges in two to four steps in our experiments (Section~\ref{section:results_exact}).

\paragraph{The smallest eigenpair.} The start needs $m_1$ and $v_1$. They come from the characteristic polynomial $P(\lambda) = \det(M_i - \lambda I) = \prod_k (m_k - \lambda)$, whose coefficients follow from the traces of $M_i$, $M_i^2$ and $M_i^3$ and from $\det M_i$ by Newton's identities. On $(-\infty, m_1]$ every factor is non-negative, so $P$ is positive, decreasing and convex there, and Newton's method started from any point below $m_1$ increases monotonically to $m_1$, by the same tangent argument. Since $M_i$ is positive semi-definite, $\lambda = 0$ is such a point. The eigenvector follows from the adjugate: $(M_i - m_1 I) \, \mathrm{adj}(M_i - m_1 I) = P(m_1) \, I = 0$, so every column of the adjugate is a multiple of $v_1$, and the column $k$ has length proportional to $|v_{1,k}|$. Taking the column with the largest diagonal entry, the largest coordinate of $v_1$, avoids the columns that vanish, and gives $v_1$ to full accuracy.

\paragraph{The hard case.} When the pull is orthogonal to the cell's own rotation, $c_1 = v_1 \cdot h_i = 0$, the pole at $m_1$ disappears and $|R(\lambda)|$ stays bounded as $\lambda \to m_1$. If its limit $|y|$, with $y = \sum_{k \geq 2} c_k v_k / (m_k - m_1)$, is less than one, the multiplier stops at $\lambda^* = m_1$ and the minimizer is $R^*_i = y \pm \sqrt{1 - |y|^2} \, v_1$: the part along $v_1$ is no longer determined by the pull, only completed to unit length. This \emph{hard case} of the trust-region literature \cite{MoreSorensen1983} looks like a curiosity, but Section~\ref{section:hard_case} shows that it is exactly what happens when a handle is turned by $180^\circ$.

\paragraph{Two limits.} The exact cell interpolates between two familiar answers. Without bending, $\alpha A \to 0$, the pull vanishes, $\lambda^* \to m_1$, and $R^*_i \to \pm v_1$: the ARAP rotation of the cell, with the sign chosen by the side of the pull, which also resolves the ambiguity between $R$ and $-R$. With very stiff bending, $\alpha A \to \infty$, the membrane term becomes negligible, $\lambda^* \approx -|h_i|$, and $R^*_i \to g_i / |g_i|$: the normalized mean of the neighbors. The linearized cell has the same two limits whenever the neighbors agree, $|g_i| = 1$, which is another way to see why it works.

\subsection{Constrained cells and large rotations}
\label{section:hard_case}

Section~\ref{section:rotation_constraints} gave the cells inside the handles the rotation of the cell equation without feedback, (\ref{eq:rotation_constraint}): the fit of their edges, which the handle moves rigidly, smoothed towards the previous rotations of their neighbors. That rule works well as long as the handles turn by moderate angles, and it fails, in a precise sense, when a handle is turned by a large angle at once: the cells of the handle do not follow the handle. This section explains why, and derives the rule we use instead. The explanation needs two notions, the cell's own rotation and the pull of its neighbors, and one fact about quaternions.

\paragraph{Notation.} Throughout this section $i \in \mathcal C$ is a constrained cell, and $M_i = \sum_{k=1}^4 m_k \, v_k v_k^T$ is the eigendecomposition of its membrane matrix, with $0 \leq m_1 \leq m_2 \leq m_3 \leq m_4$ and orthonormal $v_k$ (we omit the index $i$ on $m_k$ and $v_k$).
\begin{defn}
\label{defn:pull}
The \emph{own fit} of cell $i$ is the unit quaternion $v_1 = v_1(M_i)$, defined up to sign: the rotation that fits the cell's edges best, the minimizer of the membrane term $R^T M_i R$ on the unit sphere. The \emph{pull} of its neighbors is the right-hand side of (\ref{eq:rotation_constraint}),
\begin{equation*}
h_i = \alpha A \, \bar g_i, \qquad \bar g_i = \frac{1}{d_i} \sum_{j \in N(i)} R^{prev}_j ,
\end{equation*}
and its components in the eigenbasis are $c_k = v_k \cdot h_i$. The \emph{pull angle} of the cell is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pull_angle}
\theta_i = 2 \arccos \frac{|v_1 \cdot h_i|}{|h_i|} \in [0^\circ, 180^\circ] .
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

The pull angle has a geometric meaning, which the following elementary fact makes precise.
\begin{prop}
\label{prop:quaternion_angle}
Let $q_1$ and $q_2$ be unit quaternions, and let $\theta \in [0^\circ, 180^\circ]$ be the angle of the rotation that takes the rotation of $q_1$ to that of $q_2$. Then $|q_1 \cdot q_2| = \cos(\theta / 2)$, where $q_1 \cdot q_2$ is the Euclidean inner product of their coefficients in $\mathbb R^4$.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
The relative rotation is $\tilde q_1 q_2$. Writing $q_k = w_k + B_k$ as in Section~\ref{section:the_form_of_arap_matrix}, the scalar part of $\tilde q_1 q_2 = (w_1 - B_1)(w_2 + B_2)$ is $w_1 w_2 + B_1 \cdot B_2 = q_1 \cdot q_2$, since the scalar part of $-B_1 B_2$ is $B_1 \cdot B_2$. A unit quaternion that rotates by $\theta$ has scalar part $\pm \cos(\theta/2)$, the sign depending on which of the two quaternions of the rotation is taken.
\end{proof}
So $\theta_i$ is the angle, as a rotation, between the cell's own fit and the mean rotation of its neighbors (normalized). Two rotations $180^\circ$ apart have \emph{orthogonal} quaternions; this is the situation of a handle turned by $180^\circ$, whose cells' own fit is the handle's rotation, while their neighbors, outside the handle, are still at rest.

\paragraph{What the cell equation does with a large pull angle.} Expanding the solution of (\ref{eq:rotation_constraint}) in the eigenbasis of $M_i$ shows exactly how much the smoothing takes the cell away from its own fit.
\begin{prop}
\label{prop:lag}
Let $R = (M_i + \alpha A \, I)^{-1} h_i$ be the unnormalized solution of the cell equation (\ref{eq:rotation_constraint}), and $\beta \in [0^\circ, 90^\circ]$ the angle between $R$ and the line of $v_1$ in $\mathbb R^4$, so that the normalized $R$ differs from the own fit by a rotation of $2 \beta$. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lag}
\frac{m_1 + \alpha A}{m_4 + \alpha A} \, \tan \frac{\theta_i}{2} \;\leq\; \tan \beta \;\leq\; \frac{m_1 + \alpha A}{m_2 + \alpha A} \, \tan \frac{\theta_i}{2} .
\end{equation}
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
In the eigenbasis, $R = \sum_k \frac{c_k}{m_k + \alpha A} \, v_k$. Its component along $v_1$ has length $|c_1| / (m_1 + \alpha A)$, and its component orthogonal to $v_1$ has squared length $\sum_{k \geq 2} c_k^2 / (m_k + \alpha A)^2$, which lies between $\sum_{k \geq 2} c_k^2 / (m_4 + \alpha A)^2$ and $\sum_{k \geq 2} c_k^2 / (m_2 + \alpha A)^2$. By the definition (\ref{eq:pull_angle}), $|c_1| = |h_i| \cos(\theta_i / 2)$, and since the $v_k$ are orthonormal, $\sum_{k \geq 2} c_k^2 = |h_i|^2 - c_1^2 = |h_i|^2 \sin^2(\theta_i / 2)$. Dividing the orthogonal length by the parallel one gives $\tan \beta$ between the two bounds.
\end{proof}

What matters for the iteration is not the lag itself but how it changes from one local step to the next. At the start of a local step the cell sits, roughly, at the mean rotation of its neighbors, whose quaternion line makes the angle $\theta_i / 2$ with that of the own fit (Proposition~\ref{prop:quaternion_angle}); the cell equation takes it to the angle $\beta$. The difference
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:progress}
\delta_i = \frac{\theta_i}{2} - \beta
\end{equation}
is the \emph{progress} of the cell towards its own fit during one local step, measured as an angle between quaternion lines (the rotation angle is twice as large). Proposition~\ref{prop:lag} bounds it between two functions of the same shape.
\begin{prop}
\label{prop:progress}
Let $r_2 = (m_1 + \alpha A)/(m_2 + \alpha A)$ and $r_4 = (m_1 + \alpha A)/(m_4 + \alpha A)$, both in $(0, 1]$, and for $r \in (0, 1]$ let $f_r(x) = x - \arctan(r \tan x)$ on $[0^\circ, 90^\circ]$. Then
\begin{equation*}
f_{r_2}(\theta_i / 2) \;\leq\; \delta_i \;\leq\; f_{r_4}(\theta_i / 2).
\end{equation*}
Each $f_r$ vanishes at $x = 0^\circ$ and at $x = 90^\circ$, is positive in between when $r < 1$, and increases up to $\tan x = 1 / \sqrt r$, then decreases. In particular the progress is zero at $\theta_i = 180^\circ$, whatever the stiffness.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
The bounds follow from (\ref{eq:lag}) because $\arctan$ is increasing. Clearly $f_r(0) = 0$, and $r \tan x \to \infty$ as $x \to 90^\circ$, so $\arctan(r \tan x) \to 90^\circ$ and $f_r \to 0$. For $r < 1$, $r \tan x < \tan x$ gives $f_r > 0$ in between. Differentiating, $f_r'(x) = 1 - r (1 + \tan^2 x) / (1 + r^2 \tan^2 x)$, which vanishes when $1 + r^2 t^2 = r (1 + t^2)$ with $t = \tan x$, that is when $t^2 (r - r^2) = 1 - r$, or $t^2 = 1/r$; $f_r'$ is positive before that point and negative after it.
\end{proof}

On our meshes the bending stiffness dominates the cells' own stiffness, $m_k \ll \alpha A$: the ratios $r_2$ and $r_4$ of the handle cells of the bar lie between $0.985$ and $0.999$, and on the Armadillo's control mesh, whose cells are larger, $r_4$ goes down to $0.38$ (Section~\ref{section:results_exact}). The cell equation then returns almost the mean of the neighbors, and moves the cell only a small step towards its own fit in each local step. The handle reaches its cells through those small steps, and through the global step, iteration after iteration. Proposition~\ref{prop:progress} says how the size of the step depends on the angle: it grows with $\theta_i$ up to about $90^\circ$ (exactly, up to $\tan(\theta_i/2) = 1/\sqrt r$, which is $90^\circ$ for $r = 1$ and a little more for $r < 1$), and beyond that it shrinks, until at $\theta_i = 180^\circ$ it vanishes. A handle turned by $180^\circ$ at once is therefore the worst case: its cells do not move towards the handle at all, and they stay at the rotation of their neighbors. The exact cell on the sphere (Section~\ref{section:exact_cell}) does not help here: at $\theta_i = 180^\circ$ the pull meets the condition $c_1 = 0$ of the hard case, and the exact cell agrees with the linearized one to $10^{-3}$ degrees in our experiments (Table~\ref{tab:exact_cells}).

The consequence for the deformation is visible in Figure~\ref{fig:hard_case}(a): when a handle is turned by $180^\circ$ at once, its cells stay close to their neighbors' rotation, and the bar is pinched just below the handle. The rotation field is in a balanced but unstable configuration, which it leaves only when round-off and the slow change of the positions break the symmetry; on the bar this takes between $1{,}000$ and $2{,}000$ local/global iterations (Section~\ref{section:results_exact}).

\paragraph{The adaptive rule.} The diagnosis suggests the remedy. The rotation of a constrained cell is not an unknown of the local step, and nothing obliges us to smooth it: the smoothing exists to make the handle blend into its surroundings, and Propositions~\ref{prop:lag} and~\ref{prop:progress} show that beyond $\theta_i = 90^\circ$ it does the opposite of blending: the further the pull is from the own fit, the more slowly the cell is brought to its handle, and at $180^\circ$ it is not brought at all. We therefore keep the cell equation where it behaves well and take the own fit where it does not:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:own_fit}
R^{const}_i =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{(M_i + \alpha A \, I)^{-1} h_i}{|(M_i + \alpha A \, I)^{-1} h_i|} & \text{if } \theta_i \leq \theta_{\max}, \\[2ex]
\sigma_i \, v_1(M_i) & \text{if } \theta_i > \theta_{\max},
\end{cases}
\qquad \theta_{\max} = 90^\circ .
\end{equation}
Three choices in (\ref{eq:own_fit}) need a justification.

\emph{The threshold.} By Proposition~\ref{prop:progress}, the progress of the cell equation towards the own fit is largest near $\theta_i = 90^\circ$ and decreases beyond it, to zero at $180^\circ$; the further the pull is beyond $90^\circ$, the slower the cell equation brings the cell to its handle. $\theta_{\max} = 90^\circ$ is therefore the angle beyond which the cell equation stops being the fastest way for the cell to reach its own fit, and taking the own fit directly can only help. In $\mathbb R^4$ it is the angle at which the pull makes $45^\circ$ with the line of the own fit: it is as close to the own fit as to the space orthogonal to it. The experiments of Section~\ref{section:results_exact} show that the choice is not critical.

\emph{The sign.} A quaternion and its opposite represent the same rotation, but the bending term $\|R_i - R_j\|^2$ of (\ref{eq:local_energy}) compares them as vectors of $\mathbb R^4$, so neighboring cells must use quaternions on the same side. Choosing $\sigma_i = \pm 1$ from the cell's previous rotation, as elsewhere, fails precisely here: when $\theta_i > 90^\circ$ the previous rotation is nearly orthogonal to $v_1$, and the sign of $v_1 \cdot R^{prev}_i$ is decided by round-off, differently in neighboring cells. Instead we take $\sigma_i$ so that $\sigma_i \, v_1 \cdot q_H \geq 0$, where $q_H$ is the quaternion of the handle that contains the vertex. The handle is turned by the user in small increments, so $q_H$ moves continuously and stays on one branch, and all the cells of a handle get the same side.

\emph{The fixed point.} The rule changes the path of the iterations, not their destination.
\begin{prop}
\label{prop:same_fixed_point}
Let $(\mathrm P, \mathrm R)$ be a state of the local/global iteration at which every constrained cell has $\theta_i \leq \theta_{\max}$. Then the cell equation and the adaptive rule (\ref{eq:own_fit}) give the same constrained rotations at $(\mathrm P, \mathrm R)$, and hence the same local and global steps. In particular, such a state is a fixed point of the iteration with the adaptive rule if and only if it is a fixed point with the cell equation.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
For $\theta_i \leq \theta_{\max}$ the first case of (\ref{eq:own_fit}) is (\ref{eq:rotation_constraint}) itself. The constrained rotations are the only place where the two iterations differ, so they produce the same rotation system (\ref{eqn:local_reduced}), the same rotations, and the same positions.
\end{proof}
At the fixed points we reach, every handle cell has its own fit within $27^\circ$ of its neighbors' mean, far below $\theta_{\max}$ (Section~\ref{section:results_exact}), so the proposition applies: the adaptive rule converges to the same shapes as the cell equation, only sooner. When the handles turn gradually, as in interactive editing, the neighbors follow the handle from one frame to the next, the pull angle stays small, and the rule never departs from the cell equation.

\paragraph{Cost.} The rule needs the own fit $v_1$ of every constrained cell, which is the smallest eigenpair of a $4 \times 4$ matrix, computed with the Newton iteration and the adjugate column of Section~\ref{section:exact_cell}, a few dozen operations per cell; the pull $h_i$ is already on the right-hand side of (\ref{eq:rotation_constraint}). On the bar, with 1{,}898 constrained cells, the rule adds $0.6$~ms to a PCG local step of $14.7$~ms.

\section{Multiresolution Optimization}

To achieve interactive rates, we optimize the SR-ARAP energy on a low-resolution version of the input mesh, the \emph{control mesh}, and then transfer the solution to the full-resolution mesh. The control mesh is obtained by simplifying the input mesh with \emph{half-edge collapses} that minimize the quadric error metric, so that it is a triangulation of a subset of the original vertices. Each control vertex $k$ is associated with a full-resolution vertex $h(k)$: the vertex it was kept from, or equivalently its nearest full-resolution vertex.

Once the optimal deformed shape of the control mesh is found, the optimized rotations are transferred to the full-resolution mesh by harmonic interpolation of quaternions (see Section~\ref{section:harmonic_interpolation_rotors}). The full-resolution positions are then obtained from Equation~(\ref{eq:ls_for_p_ga}) on the full-resolution mesh, with the interpolated rotations and with the optimized control vertices as positional constraints, $p_{h(k)} = p^{control}_k$:
\begin{equation*}
\sum_{j \in N(i)} c_{ij} \, (p_j - p_i) = \sum_{j \in N(i)} \frac{c_{ij}}{2} \, \big( R_i \, q_{ij} \, \tilde R_i + R_j \, q_{ij} \, \tilde R_j \big).
\end{equation*}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:frame} summarizes one interactive frame.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{One interactive frame. The Cholesky factorizations of Sections~\ref{section:global_step} and~\ref{section:harmonic_interpolation_rotors}, and that of the preconditioner $\mathrm C$ of Section~\ref{section:pcg}, are computed once per constraint set.}
\label{alg:frame}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Set the positional constraints $p^{const}_i$ of the control mesh from the handle transformations.
\FOR{$k = 1$ to $K$}
  \STATE Compute $M_i$ for every control vertex from the current positions (Section~\ref{section:the_form_of_arap_matrix}).
  \STATE Compute $R^{const}_i$, $i \in \mathcal C$, by the adaptive rule (\ref{eq:own_fit}); solve (\ref{eqn:local_reduced}) for $R_i$, $i \in \mathcal F$, by PCG from the previous rotations; normalize all $R_i$.
  \STATE Solve the global step (\ref{eq:global_reduced}) on the control mesh.
\ENDFOR
\STATE Interpolate the control rotations onto the full-resolution mesh (\ref{eq:harmonic_reduced}) and normalize them.
\STATE Solve the global step (\ref{eq:global_reduced}) on the full-resolution mesh, constrained to the control positions.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Harmonic Interpolation of Quaternions}
\label{section:harmonic_interpolation_rotors}

The seminal work of Pinkall and Polthier \cite{Pinkall1993} shows how to interpolate given data over a discrete domain using harmonic maps. Harmonic maps are critical points of the Dirichlet (stretching) energy $\int_{\Omega} |\nabla f|^2 \, dA$, which generates minimal surfaces.
The \emph{discrete harmonic energy} of a map $f$ defined on the mesh vertices $\{q_i\}$ has the form
\begin{equation}
E(f) = \sum_{\{i,j\} \in \mathcal E} c_{ij} \, \| f(q_j) - f(q_i) \|^2 ,
\end{equation}
where $c_{ij}$ are the symmetric cotangent weights \cite{Pinkall1993} of the edges. The discrete Laplacian operator can be identified in that energy as
\begin{equation}
\Delta f(q_i) = \sum_{j \in N(i)} c_{ij} \, \big( f(q_j) - f(q_i) \big).
\end{equation}
Harmonic energy is known to minimize angular distortion, which means that harmonic functions blend boundary conditions smoothly over the domain. Harmonic functions are intrinsic to surfaces and independent of the discretization used to produce the meshes. In a spirit similar to \cite{Zayer2005}, we interpolate quaternions over a mesh with harmonic functions. The harmonic quaternion interpolation over a surface mesh is the solution of the discrete harmonic equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:harmonic_rotor}
   \sum_{j \in N(i)} c_{ij} \, ( R_j - R_i ) = 0
\end{equation}
subject to Dirichlet boundary conditions $R_{h(k)} = R^{control}_k$, where $\{R^{control}_k\}$ are the optimized quaternions of the control mesh. This interpolation produces a smooth field of quaternions by ``averaging'' the boundary conditions gradually over the surface, in effect a linear interpolation of the boundary conditions across the surface. In matrix form,
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm L \, \mathrm R = 0 \qquad \text{s.t.} \ R_{h(k)} = R^{control}_k ,
\end{equation*}
where $\mathrm L$ is the full-resolution Laplacian of the global step and $\mathrm R$ is the $m \times 4$ matrix of quaternion coefficients. Substituting the boundary conditions as in Section~\ref{section:global_step}, with $\mathcal C = \{h(k)\}$, gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:harmonic_reduced}
(-\mathrm L_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}) \, \mathrm R_{\mathcal F} = \mathrm L_{\mathcal F \mathcal C} \, \mathrm R^{control}_{\mathcal C},
\end{equation}
which has the same matrix as the full-resolution global step, whose constrained vertices are the same set $\{h(k)\}$. One Cholesky factorization therefore serves both the rotation interpolation, with four right-hand sides, and the full-resolution positions, with three. The interpolated quaternions are not of unit length and must be normalized afterwards. Despite its simplicity, this approach works surprisingly well, is extremely efficient, and provides a natural propagation at no extra cost.

 \section{The form of $M_i$}
 \label{section:the_form_of_arap_matrix}

As noted in Section~\ref{section:local_step}, for a unit quaternion $R_i$ the membrane term equals
\begin{equation*}
\sum_j c_{ij} \, \| R_i \, q_{ij} \, \tilde R_i - p_{ij} \|^2 = \sum_j c_{ij} \, \| R_i \, q_{ij} - p_{ij} \, R_i \|^2 .
\end{equation*}
We now compute the matrix $M_{ij}$ that represents $R_i \, q_{ij} - p_{ij} \, R_i$ and the matrix $M_i$ of the cell.

\paragraph{Expanding the products.} Write $R_i = w_i + B_i$, with scalar part $w_i$ and pure quaternion part $B_i$. For pure quaternions $u$ and $v$ the quaternion product is $u \, v = -u \cdot v + u \times v$, a scalar part and a vector part. Since the scalar $w_i$ commutes with everything,
\begin{equation*}
R_i \, q_{ij} = -B_i \cdot q_{ij} + w_i \, q_{ij} + B_i \times q_{ij},
\qquad
p_{ij} \, R_i = -p_{ij} \cdot B_i + w_i \, p_{ij} + p_{ij} \times B_i .
\end{equation*}
Subtracting, and using $B \times q + B \times p = -(q + p) \times B$,
\begin{equation}
     R_i \, q_{ij} - p_{ij} \, R_i = -\, a_{ij} \cdot B_i + w_i \, a_{ij} - b_{ij} \times B_i ,
     \qquad a_{ij} = q_{ij} - p_{ij}, \quad b_{ij} = q_{ij} + p_{ij},
\end{equation}
where the first term is the scalar part and the last two are the vector part.

\paragraph{The matrix $M_{ij}$.} Representing $R_i$ as the $4 \times 1$ column vector made of the scalar $w_i$ and the $3 \times 1$ column vector $B_i$, the same expression in matrix form is
\begin{equation}
	M_{ij} R_i =
	\left[\begin{array}{cc}
		0        &       -a_{ij}^T \\
		a_{ij}  &   -\left[ b_{ij} \right]_\times \\
	\end{array}\right]
	\left[\begin{array}{c}
		w_i \\
		B_i
	\end{array}\right] =
	\left[\begin{array}{c}
		-a_{ij}^T B_i \\
		w_i \, a_{ij} - b_{ij} \times B_i
	\end{array}\right],
\end{equation}
where $a_{ij}$ and $b_{ij}$ are $3 \times 1$ column vectors and $\left[ b \right]_\times$ is the skew-symmetric cross-product matrix, $\left[ b \right]_\times x = b \times x$. Since $\left[ b_{ij} \right]_\times$ is skew-symmetric and the two off-diagonal blocks are $-a_{ij}^T$ and $a_{ij}$, $M_{ij}$ is a skew-symmetric $4 \times 4$ real matrix, $M_{ij}^T = -M_{ij}$.

\paragraph{The product $M_{ij}^T M_{ij}$.} Multiplying the blocks of $M_{ij}^T$ and $M_{ij}$, and using $-a^T [b]_\times = ([b]_\times a)^T = (b \times a)^T$,
\begin{equation}
	M_{ij}^T M_{ij} =
	\left[\begin{array}{cc}
		\| a_{ij} \|^2       &         (b_{ij} \times a_{ij})^T \\
		b_{ij} \times a_{ij}  &    a_{ij} a_{ij}^T - \left[ b_{ij} \right]^2_\times \\
	\end{array}\right].
\end{equation}
It is symmetric and positive semi-definite, as every matrix of the form $X^T X$, and since $c_{ij} \geq 0$ so is the sum $M_i = \sum_{j} c_{ij} \, M_{ij}^T M_{ij}$ of (\ref{eq:membrane_quadratic}): all its eigenvalues are real and non-negative. Its trace, used in Section~\ref{section:pcg}, is $\mathrm{tr} \, M_{ij}^T M_{ij} = 2 \|a_{ij}\|^2 + 2 \|b_{ij}\|^2 = 4 (\|p_{ij}\|^2 + \|q_{ij}\|^2)$, since $\mathrm{tr}(-[b]_\times^2) = 2 \|b\|^2$.


 \subsection{Efficient Computation of $M_i$}

Since $\left[ b \right]^2_\times = b \, b^T - \| b \|^2 I$, the product $M_{ij}^T M_{ij}$ has the simple form
\begin{equation*}
	M_{ij}^T M_{ij} =
	\left[\begin{array}{cc}
		\| a_{ij} \|^2        &         (b_{ij} \times a_{ij})^T \\
		b_{ij} \times a_{ij}  &    a_{ij} a_{ij}^T - b_{ij} b_{ij}^T + \| b_{ij} \|^2 I_{3\times3} \\
	\end{array}\right].
\end{equation*}
Writing it in terms of $q_{ij}$ and $p_{ij}$, with $\|a_{ij}\|^2 = \|q_{ij}\|^2 + \|p_{ij}\|^2 - 2 \, q_{ij}^T p_{ij}$, $\|b_{ij}\|^2 = \|q_{ij}\|^2 + \|p_{ij}\|^2 + 2 \, q_{ij}^T p_{ij}$, $b_{ij} \times a_{ij} = -2 \, q_{ij} \times p_{ij}$ and $a_{ij} a_{ij}^T - b_{ij} b_{ij}^T = -2 \, (q_{ij} p_{ij}^T + p_{ij} q_{ij}^T)$, gives
\begin{equation}
   \label{eqn:matrix_fast}
   \begin{aligned}
   	M_{ij}^T M_{ij}
   	= {} & -2
	\left[\begin{array}{cc}
		q_{ij}^T p_{ij}       &         (q_{ij} \times p_{ij})^T \\
		q_{ij} \times p_{ij}  &    q_{ij} p_{ij}^T + p_{ij} q_{ij}^T - q_{ij}^T p_{ij} \, I_{3\times3} \\
	\end{array}\right] \\
    & + (\| p_{ij} \|^2 + \| q_{ij} \|^2) \, I_{4\times4}.
   \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
All terms of (\ref{eqn:matrix_fast}) can be derived from the dyadic tensor $q_{ij} p_{ij}^T$ and the scalar $\|p_{ij}\|^2 + \|q_{ij}\|^2$. Summing over the cell, $M_i$ is obtained from the single $3 \times 3$ matrix $T_i = \sum_j c_{ij} \, q_{ij} p_{ij}^T$ and the scalar $\sigma_i = \sum_j c_{ij} (\|p_{ij}\|^2 + \|q_{ij}\|^2)$: the trace of $T_i$ gives $\sum_j c_{ij} \, q_{ij}^T p_{ij}$, its antisymmetric part gives $\sum_j c_{ij} \, q_{ij} \times p_{ij}$, and $T_i + T_i^T$ gives $\sum_j c_{ij} (q_{ij} p_{ij}^T + p_{ij} q_{ij}^T)$. Computing $M_i$ therefore costs one $3 \times 3$ outer-product accumulation per edge.

\section{Implementation}
\label{section:implementation}

The author's source code is publicly available on GitHub \cite{GALinearizedArap}. The implementation divides Equations (\ref{eqn:local_solution_feedback}) and (\ref{eq:rotation_constraint}) by two, working with $\frac{1}{2} M_i$ and $\frac{\alpha}{2}$. It uses $\alpha = 0.28$ (that is, $\frac{\alpha}{2} = 0.14$) and $e = 0.2$, and adds $\eta = 2 \cdot 10^{-6}$ to the diagonal of every block $M_i + \alpha A \, I$ for numerical safety, which adds the Tikhonov term $\eta \sum_i (d_i + e) \, \|R_i\|^2$ to (\ref{eq:exact_energy}). The constrained rotations follow the adaptive rule (\ref{eq:own_fit}) with $\theta_{\max} = 90^\circ$. Each interactive frame performs $K = 3$ local/global iterations on the control mesh. The local step is solved by PCG (Section~\ref{section:pcg}), warm-started from the previous rotations and stopped at a relative residual of $10^{-12}$; its preconditioner $\mathrm C$ is factored when the constraints are set and reused afterwards. Convergence is monitored with the energy (\ref{eq:sr_arap}), with $w_{ij} = 1/d_i$.

\section{Results}
\label{section:results}

\paragraph{Setup.} All measurements were taken on an Apple M1 Pro laptop (10 cores, 32~GB), with a port of the reference implementation \cite{GALinearizedArap} to Rust that uses the sparse Cholesky factorization of the \texttt{faer} library; every factorization and solve runs on a single thread, except that the right-hand sides of a Poisson solve are solved in parallel. The local step is solved by PCG with the factor-once preconditioner (Section~\ref{section:pcg}); we call its solution, and that of a direct sparse Cholesky factorization of $\mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}$, the closed form, since the two agree to about $10^{-12}$~rad. Unless stated otherwise, $\alpha = 0.28$, $e = 0.2$ and $K = 3$ local/global iterations per frame (Section~\ref{section:implementation}). The models are the Armadillo (172{,}974 vertices) and a cactus (5{,}261 vertices), each with a 502-vertex control mesh, and a tube, the classic bar benchmark of ARAP papers, generated at resolutions from 530 to 124{,}010 vertices; its ends are capped by concentric rings so that no vertex has more than 12 neighbors. The relaxation baseline solves exactly the same per-cell equations (\ref{eqn:local_solution_feedback}): each sweep visits every free cell once and solves its $4 \times 4$ system with the current neighbor rotations (block Gauss--Seidel), and the quaternions are normalized after the last sweep. By Proposition~\ref{prop:exact_energy}, relaxation and the closed form share the same solution; with $e = 0.2$ we verified that relaxation reaches it to machine precision ($10^{-15}$~rad) on every mesh.

\subsection{Comparison with SR-ARAP}
\label{section:results_srarap}

We compare against the original SR-ARAP local step of Levi and Gotsman \cite{Levi2015}, run with the same global step, constraints and multiresolution transfer. It keeps exact rotation matrices: each cell's rotation maximizes $\mathrm{tr}(R_i^T S_i)$ with
\begin{equation*}
S_i = \sum_{j \in N(i)} c_{ij} \, p_{ij} q_{ij}^T + \alpha' A \sum_{j \in N(i)} (w_{ij} + w_{ji}) \, R_j ,
\end{equation*}
found by a singular value decomposition (orthogonal Procrustes) with the neighboring rotations fixed, and cells are visited in Gauss--Seidel sweeps. Each update therefore minimizes the total SR-ARAP energy, with bending term $\alpha' A \sum_i \sum_j w_{ij} \| R_i - R_j \|_F^2$, exactly over one cell. We use $w_{ij} = 1/d_i$ and $\alpha' = \alpha / 8$, which gives both bending terms the same stiffness for small rotation differences ($\|R_i - R_j\|_F^2 \approx 2 \theta^2$ and $\|q_i - q_j\|^2 \approx \theta^2 / 4$ for rotations $\theta$ apart). The two methods do not minimize the same energy, so we compare the resulting shapes with two measures that depend only on the deformed positions: the ARAP distortion $\sum c_{ij} \| R_i q_{ij} - p_{ij} \|^2 / \sum c_{ij} \|q_{ij}\|^2$ of the best rigid fit $R_i$ of every cell, and the RMS angle between the fitted rotations of adjacent cells (roughness).

Table~\ref{tab:srarap} and Figure~\ref{fig:srarap} report both measures on the bar bent into a $90^\circ$ arc and on the Armadillo, against the wall-clock time of the local/global iterations. The closed form converges to shapes with lower distortion and smoother rotations: 12\% lower distortion and $1.18^\circ$ against $1.37^\circ$ roughness on the bar, and 5\% lower distortion on the Armadillo. SR-ARAP with 3 and with 10 sweeps per iteration converges to the same shape, so the difference does not come from the sweep budget. The shapes differ by up to 2.6\% of the model's half-diagonal on the bar and 1.6\% on the Armadillo; Figure~\ref{fig:arc_comparison} shows where. Our method also gets there sooner. Measured by the wall-clock time to come within 1\% of the distortion SR-ARAP converges to, PCG takes 3.5~s on the bar, against 7.4~s for SR-ARAP with 3 or with 10 sweeps per iteration (2.1 times faster), and 5.6~ms on the Armadillo, against 24~ms for SR-ARAP with 10 sweeps and 28--30~ms with 1 or 3 (4.3 to 5.3 times faster); it then continues to the lower distortion that SR-ARAP does not reach. Counted in iterations rather than time, the picture is more nuanced: on the Armadillo the closed form is ahead from the fifth iteration on, but on the bar, which undergoes much larger rotations, it leads for the first 50 iterations and again from the 230th on; in between SR-ARAP with 10 sweeps is ahead, and it settles within 1\% of its final distortion after 160 iterations, against 540 for ours, each of its iterations costing three times as much. With a direct factorization of $\mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}$ in every iteration instead of PCG, the bar would take 18.2~s: the speed-up over SR-ARAP relies on the iterative solver of Section~\ref{section:pcg}. We attribute the slower start to the feedback term, which pulls every rotation towards its previous value: it provides temporal coherence and conditioning, at the price of a slower outer loop. The lower distortion does not come from the feedback: without it ($e = 0$) the closed form converges to an even lower distortion on the bar, $3.38 \cdot 10^{-3}$.

An SR-ARAP sweep costs one $3 \times 3$ SVD per cell, about ten times a sweep of the linearized relaxation (4.8 against 0.6~ms on the bar). A PCG local step costs about as much as three SR-ARAP sweeps (14.3 against 13.5~ms on the bar, 0.88 against 0.97~ms on the Armadillo's control mesh), but it is exact. Inexact relaxation of our linearized system with 3 sweeps per iteration is faster still (0.6~s on the bar), but its rotations are not exact.

\subsection{Closed form and relaxation}

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}(a) shows how far relaxation is from the closed-form solution after each sweep, in the hardest case for relaxation: the first local step after the handle of a tube is bent by $60^\circ$, with all rotations still at rest. With the budget of two or three sweeps that interactive SR-ARAP implementations use, the rotations are off by up to $0.11$~rad ($6.6^\circ$) on the coarsest tube and $5 \cdot 10^{-3}$~rad on the finest; the closed form has no such error.

The number of sweeps needed to reach a given accuracy, however, does not grow with the mesh: 25 to 50 sweeps reach $10^{-3}$~rad at every resolution. The reason is the feedback term. By Proposition~\ref{prop:rotation_spd}, the eigenvalues of $\mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}$ are bounded below by $e$, independently of the mesh, so the system is well conditioned and relaxation contracts at a mesh-independent rate. Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}(b) confirms this: with $e = 0.2$ the sweep count stays flat, while with weaker feedback it grows, and without feedback ($e = 0$, the plain smooth-rotation system) it grows linearly with the number of vertices, from 35 sweeps at 530 vertices to 2{,}022 at 31{,}274.

\subsection{Solving the rotation system: direct and iterative}
\label{section:results_solvers}

Table~\ref{tab:solvers} and Figure~\ref{fig:cost} compare three ways of computing the same exact local step on the bent tube, in the cold start of Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}: a direct sparse Cholesky factorization of $\mathrm K_{\mathcal F \mathcal F}$ in every local step (with the symbolic analysis reused), conjugate gradients without a preconditioner, and PCG with the factor-once preconditioner $\mathrm C$ of Section~\ref{section:pcg}. Both iterative solvers start from the previous rotations and are run until their rotations are within $10^{-12}$~rad of the direct solution.

The direct factorization grows superlinearly, roughly as $n^{1.5}$: from 1.8~ms at 530 vertices to 84~ms at 7{,}946 and 5.7~s at 124{,}010. Conjugate gradients needs 59 to 77 iterations at every resolution, as the first bound of Proposition~\ref{prop:conditioning} predicts, and its cost grows linearly, as $n^{0.94}$, reaching 0.31~s at 124{,}010 vertices. PCG needs even fewer iterations as the mesh is refined, 18 at 530 vertices and 5 at 124{,}010, as the second bound predicts; with each iteration costing one back-substitution, it solves the local step in 1.1~ms at 530 vertices and 0.11~s at 124{,}010, 1.7 to 49 times faster than the direct factorization. The factorization of $\mathrm C$ itself takes 0.4 to 110~ms and is done once per constraint set. From about 8{,}000 vertices on, PCG reaches the exact solution in the time relaxation needs for an accuracy of $10^{-3}$~rad (Figure~\ref{fig:cost}); on the smallest tubes relaxation to that accuracy remains a few times cheaper; preconditioning with the inverses of the $4 \times 4$ diagonal blocks (block Jacobi) brings no improvement over unpreconditioned conjugate gradients.

With feedback, relaxation also converges in a mesh-independent number of sweeps, but only to the accuracy it is given time for. Without feedback ($e = 0$) its sweep count grows linearly with the mesh (Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}(b)), while the second bound of Proposition~\ref{prop:conditioning} no longer holds; the first bound degenerates as $e \to 0$ as well, and a direct factorization, which needs no bound on the conditioning, remains available.

\subsection{Effect on the optimization}

Figure~\ref{fig:energy} follows the SR-ARAP energy (\ref{eq:sr_arap}) through 300 local/global iterations on the bent tube (7{,}946 vertices) and on the Armadillo. On the tube, the closed form gives the lowest energy at every iteration: after 30 iterations the energy is 0.524 with the closed form, against 0.531, 0.594 and 0.693 with 10, 3 and 1 relaxation sweeps per iteration; after 300 iterations relaxation with one sweep is still 26\% above the closed form. On the 502-vertex control mesh of the Armadillo, the closed form leads during the first ten iterations, after which all methods agree to within 0.1\% (0.04\% after 300 iterations). Since the closed form minimizes $\tilde E$ rather than $E$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:exact_energy}), relaxation with a few sweeps can end slightly below it in $E$.

\subsection{Feedback and large twists}
\label{section:results_twist}

To see what the feedback contributes for large rotations, we twist the top of the bar by $270^\circ$. A handle rotated by $270^\circ$ places its vertices exactly where a rotation by $-90^\circ$ would, so its vertex positions cannot tell the two apart. Its quaternion can: $q_{270^\circ} = \cos 135^\circ + \sin 135^\circ \, n = -(\cos 45^\circ - \sin 45^\circ \, n) = -q_{-90^\circ}$ for the axis $n$, the same rotation with the opposite sign, and the bending term, which compares quaternions in $\mathbb R^4$, sees the difference. Set at once, the handle is $90^\circ$ away, as a rotation, from its resting neighbors (the rotations by $270^\circ$ and by $-90^\circ$ coincide), so its cells start right at the threshold of the adaptive rule: after the first global step their pull angles reach $95.6^\circ$. Those above $90^\circ$ take their own fit with the sign of the handle's quaternion, $q_{270^\circ}$, and the twist follows the $270^\circ$ branch (Figure~\ref{fig:twist270}(c)). This outcome is not robust: with $\theta_{\max} = 100^\circ$, or with the cell equation alone, no cell takes the handle's quaternion and the solver produces the $-90^\circ$ twist, which is an equally valid answer to a request that the positions alone cannot disambiguate. In interactive use the handle reaches $270^\circ$ gradually. We turn it by $3^\circ$ per step, with 20 local/global iterations per step, and then hold it at $270^\circ$ for another 1{,}000 iterations, with and without feedback.

With feedback ($e = 0.2$) the twist follows the handle all the way to $270^\circ$ and spreads evenly along the bar: its angle grows almost linearly with height, from $19^\circ$ in the lowest tenth of the bar to $251^\circ$ in the highest (Figure~\ref{fig:twist270}(a) and (d)). Without feedback ($e = 0$) the rotations also follow the $270^\circ$ branch, since they are warm-started from the previous step, but the twist bunches in the middle of the bar: between 35\% and 65\% of the height it grows by $144^\circ$, against $85^\circ$ with feedback and $81^\circ$ for a uniform twist. The feedback thus does more than keep the branch: by anchoring every cell to its previous rotation, it lets the twist distribute along the bar as the handle turns, instead of concentrating where the rotations change fastest.

\subsection{Exact cells and large rotations}
\label{section:results_exact}

\paragraph{How good is the frozen multiplier?} Table~\ref{tab:exact_cells} compares, cell by cell, the linearized cell equation with the exact cell on the unit sphere of Section~\ref{section:exact_cell}, at the same state: five local/global iterations into the solves of the bar bent into a $90^\circ$ arc, of the bar twisted by $180^\circ$, and of the Armadillo; and, for the twisted bar, also the cells inside the handle at the first local step, right after the handle has turned. The true multiplier is within $0.03\%$ of the frozen value $-\alpha A$ in the median free cell and within $0.4\%$ in the median handle cell, and within $3\%$ in the worst cell, the Armadillo's most deformed one, as Proposition~\ref{prop:multiplier} predicts for nearly rigid cells. The rotations are closer still: the normalized linearized cell differs from the exact one by at most $0.15^\circ$, and in the median cell by less than $10^{-4}$ degrees. The same holds in the hard case at the handle, where the pull is orthogonal to the cell's own rotation. The linearization therefore does not change the cell problem in any significant way, which confirms with an exact reference what Section~\ref{section:linearization} assumed. The exact solve itself is cheap, two to three Newton steps for the multiplier after three to five for the smallest eigenvalue: three Gauss--Seidel sweeps of exact cells cost $7.8$~ms on the bar and $0.48$~ms on the Armadillo's control mesh, against $14.7$ and $0.84$~ms for the PCG local step and $1.2$ and $0.08$~ms for three sweeps of the linearized relaxation. Since the results are the same, we keep the linearized local step.

\paragraph{The handle cells.} The exact cells do not make large twists converge faster: with three exact sweeps per local step the $180^\circ$ twist of the bar still takes about $2{,}500$ iterations to settle (Figure~\ref{fig:exact_convergence}). As Section~\ref{section:hard_case} explains, the slow part is not the linearization but the treatment of the handle cells, and the measurements bear out each step of that explanation. Right after the handle of the bar turns by $180^\circ$, half of its 1{,}898 cells (949) have a pull angle above $90^\circ$, and the largest is $180^\circ$: their own fit and the mean of their neighbors are orthogonal quaternions. Their stiffness ratios $r_2$ and $r_4$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:progress} lie between $0.985$ and $0.999$, so the cell equation can only move them by a tiny step towards the handle, and at $180^\circ$ by none at all. At the fixed point, by contrast, the largest pull angle of a handle cell is $18^\circ$ on the twisted bar, $26^\circ$ on the twisted arc, $10^\circ$ on the $90^\circ$ arc and $27^\circ$ on the Armadillo, far below $\theta_{\max}$, which is what Proposition~\ref{prop:same_fixed_point} needs.

To measure what the adaptive rule (\ref{eq:own_fit}), which our method uses, contributes, we compare it with the cell equation (\ref{eq:rotation_constraint}) alone, on four scenes set at once and solved with the PCG local step: the twisted bar, the bar bent into a $180^\circ$ arc and twisted by $180^\circ$, the $90^\circ$ arc and the Armadillo (Table~\ref{tab:adaptive} and Figure~\ref{fig:exact_convergence}). We count the iterations until the SR-ARAP energy stays within $1\%$ of its final value. On the twisted bar the adaptive rule needs 390 iterations instead of 2{,}030, and on the twisted arc 420 instead of 2{,}230: five times fewer. On the $90^\circ$ arc, whose handle turns by $90^\circ$ and whose handle cells therefore start near the threshold (377 of them above it), the rule acts during the first iterations and saves a fifth of them. On the Armadillo no handle cell ever exceeds $45^\circ$, the rule never departs from the cell equation, and the results are identical. In every case the two rules reach the same fixed point, as Proposition~\ref{prop:same_fixed_point} predicts: the final energies agree to four digits, except on the twisted arc, where after 3{,}000 iterations the cell equation alone is still $0.2\%$ above, and still decreasing. Figure~\ref{fig:hard_case} shows what the user sees: after 100 iterations the twisted bar is still pinched under the handle with the cell equation alone, and evenly twisted with the adaptive rule; after 300, the twisted arc is still flattened, and round with the adaptive rule. The threshold hardly matters: for $\theta_{\max}$ between $45^\circ$ and $150^\circ$ the twisted bar settles after the same 383 iterations, and the $90^\circ$ arc after 358 to 691, always at the same energy. The rule costs one smallest-eigenvector computation per handle cell, which adds $0.6$~ms to the $14.7$~ms of a PCG local step on the bar.

\section{Conclusion}

We have shown that the local step of SR-ARAP, when rotations are linearized as quaternions, can be solved exactly rather than by relaxation. Scaling the rows of the coupled per-cell equations makes them a single sparse, symmetric positive definite system, and that system is the exact stationarity condition of a quadratic energy, $\tilde E$, which differs from the linearized SR-ARAP energy by a valence weighting of the membrane term and an explicit proximal feedback term. The closed form is therefore not an approximation of relaxation: it is the minimizer that relaxation converges to. The same reduction makes every Poisson solve of the method symmetric positive definite, so the whole pipeline runs on sparse Cholesky factorizations without least-squares formulations.

The rotation system is also well conditioned. The feedback term bounds its smallest eigenvalue by the feedback weight, and its position-dependent part is small, of order $1/n$ relative to the rest; preconditioning with a Cholesky factorization of the position-independent part, factored once per set of constraints, bounds the condition number by $1+\mu/e$, a constant that shrinks as the mesh is refined. Conjugate gradients with this preconditioner reach the exact rotations, to $10^{-12}$~rad, in 5 to 20 iterations, each costing a sparse product and one back-substitution. The exact local step therefore costs nearly linear time: on the largest tube it is 49 times faster than a direct factorization and as fast as a few relaxation sweeps, which are not exact.

The experiments support three further conclusions. First, exactness matters at the iteration budgets used in practice: two or three relaxation sweeps leave rotation errors of several degrees, and limited sweeps slow the decrease of the energy. Second, the converged shapes are of high quality: compared with the original SR-ARAP local step they have 5--12\% lower ARAP distortion and smoother rotations, and they reach the distortion at which SR-ARAP stops 2.1 times sooner on a strongly bent bar and 4.3 times sooner on the Armadillo. Third, in the multiresolution setting the exact local step is a small part of an interactive frame on a 173{,}000-vertex model, 0.9~ms on its 502-vertex control mesh.

The feedback term is part of the contribution. It gives temporal coherence while editing, favors the branch that large rotations are following, and bounds the smallest eigenvalue of the rotation system for any mesh and constraint set, while changing the converged shape by only a fraction of a percent. Its cost is a slower outer loop for very large rotations: on the bent bar the original SR-ARAP approaches its final shape in fewer local/global iterations, although it stops at a higher distortion, and our method is faster in wall-clock time only because each of its iterations is cheaper.

Finally, the linearization is a frozen Lagrange multiplier. Solving each cell exactly on the unit sphere, through its secular equation, shows that the true multiplier exceeds the frozen one by the cell's distortion and by the disagreement of its neighbors, which in our experiments keeps the linearized rotations within $0.15^\circ$ of the exact ones. The same analysis locates the slow convergence of large twists, which the exact cells do not cure either: a handle turned by $180^\circ$ has a rotation orthogonal, as a quaternion, to those of its neighbors, and the smoothing of the handle cells keeps them at rest. Our method therefore lets those cells take their own rotation in that case, and only then: the adaptive rule, which provably leaves the fixed points unchanged and reaches the same shapes five times sooner on the twisted bar and on the twisted arc. An extension of the derivation to volumetric meshes is a natural direction for future work.
